# Legal capacity and independent oversight

Research cutoff and retrieval: **2026-10-08 UTC**. Author: Work Aiden / bounded `military_legal_checks` specialist. RC-005 candidate material; no canonical admission. This is institutional research, not legal advice.

The useful question is whether personnel and organizational changes leave servicemembers and commanders with effective, independent legal advice and review. The record supports investigating changed leadership, temporary diversions of lawyers, and oversight arrangements. It does **not** establish that ORA caused these changes, that military legal review has disappeared, or that replacement officers will subordinate law to a religious agenda.

Source IDs resolve to exact URLs, access limitations and locators in [legal_sources.json](legal_sources.json). A search-index excerpt is labeled as such; it is not treated as a retrieved full document.

## The baseline and its limits

Before 2025, Army TJAG appointment required presidential nomination, Senate consent, a qualifying legal career and an officer-board recommendation. The statute already prohibited interference with independent advice by the TJAG and unit judge advocates. This is a legal obligation, not evidence that every command honored it. [J01]

Article 6 also provided direct communication between convening authorities and their legal advisers, and between advisers and superior legal offices; it authorized qualifying civil-office details, including civil and criminal representation. Consequently, sending a military lawyer to civilian work is not intrinsically a new practice. The meaningful comparisons are mission, scale, duration, supervision and what capacity remains behind. [J02]

## Leadership: distinguish removal, vacancy, nomination and assumption

In February 2025 Hegseth defended replacing top lawyers as a matter of suitability and sought nominees for all three service TJAG offices. Contemporary AP reporting described the shake-up broadly. [J04] A crucial correction: the Navy office was already vacant. A Navy announcement dated January 2 says Christopher French retired voluntarily on January 1 and Lia Reynolds was acting. Thus three nomination requests must not become three contemporaneous removals of sitting TJAGs. [J05]

The Army's present official page records Bobby Christine assuming duties on July 1, 2025. [J06] The Air Force's indexed official biography records Christopher Eason beginning in February 2026; its full page failed to open here, so treat that date as provisional pending a saved primary copy. [J07] The June 24, 2025 Navy release records David Bligh's **nomination**, not confirmation or assumption. [J08]

This sample does not reconstruct every acting interval, all predecessor orders or individual selection-board records. It therefore cannot quantify lost advice during vacancies. Leadership replacement alone also does not prove that statutory duties, subordinate counsel, courts or complaint routes ceased operating.

## Civilian details: ceilings are not deployed headcounts

The August 28, 2025 EOIR rule broadened temporary immigration-judge eligibility to attorneys generally. The older rule allowed specified former adjudicators and certain DOJ attorneys with ten years of immigration experience; that experience condition did not apply to every old eligibility category. The new rule retained renewable six-month terms and suitable training. EOIR's stated rationale was flexibility and backlog relief, with permanent hiring continuing. [J09]

DOJ's October 23, 2025 OLC opinion describes a request for **up to 600 lawyers across phased cohorts of up to 150**, with renewable details not exceeding 179 days each. It says they would not simultaneously perform their military home jobs. Its legal conclusion depends on full-time civilian supervision; immigration-judge details were to be reimbursed, while a training purpose could justify nonreimbursed SAUSA details. This is executive-branch legal advice, not an independent court judgment or proof of compliance. [J10]

Actual EOIR notices announce **25 temporary appointments on October 24, 2025**, **27 on February 5, 2026**, and **6 on September 24, 2026**. [J11–J13] These are selected appointment events, not a complete time series, distinct-person count, present staffing level, or verified total of military detailees. Renewals, departures, permanent conversions and each person's component require reconciliation before addition. No defensible percentage of military legal capacity diverted can be calculated from this sample.

Separately, AP reported an October 27, 2025 direction to identify 48 attorneys and four paralegals for temporary DOJ assignments, with volunteering outcomes unclear. [J14] Do not add that authorization mechanically to the 600 ceiling or equate identification with completed deployments.

## Reorganization and oversight are different mechanisms

The March 11, 2026 legal-functions memorandum orders a 45-day assessment and an implementation plan for changes within six months. Its recommended division moves civilian business/support subjects toward general counsels while retaining military justice, operational law, military personnel/promotion and military-misconduct IG matters with uniformed legal leadership. It expressly preserves statutory independence and allows coordinated deviations. [J15] This is a substantial allocation review; the text does not abolish JAG review. Actual service implementation, staffing and response times remain unverified here.

A separate statute establishes a 13-member Military Justice Review Panel for independent UCMJ assessments and reports to congressional armed-services committees. [J16] In May 2026, Rosenblatt and Fidell reported that the panel had been halted in March 2025, members' service ended in April, and an internal review was being created. [J17] Their account is a serious follow-up lead, but this run did not retrieve the cessation order, final 2025 report, replacement charter or current roster. Do not equate a reported interruption with statutory repeal, or mark today's operational status verified.

## A documented counteraction

The FY2026 NDAA, enacted **December 18, 2025**, added five-day congressional notice and reasons for early removal of Army, Navy or Air Force TJAGs (§506). It also codified current legal-license requirements (§561) and sufficient qualified legal advice for operational and court-martial commanders (§562). [J03] These are real new safeguards, not pre-2025 protections. Notification is not a congressional veto; adequate staffing and enforcement must still be tested.

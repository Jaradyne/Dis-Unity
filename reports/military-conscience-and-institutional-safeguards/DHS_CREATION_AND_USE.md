# Homeland Security: creation and use

**Prepared for Jared and Digest. Evidence cutoff: 10 October 2026 UTC; this supplement was retrieved on 11 October.** This selective account extends [POSTWAR_FOUNDATIONS.md](POSTWAR_FOUNDATIONS.md). It covers twelve milestones, rather than claiming a complete DHS history or current operational census. Source IDs resolve to [dhs_sources.json](dhs_sources.json); structured dates and limitations are in [dhs_events.json](dhs_events.json).

DHS matters to the concern because it places several kinds of coercive capacity, information and emergency coordination under one Cabinet secretary. Its creation did not invent immigration enforcement or make every component part of the military. The strongest documented connections are specific: shared identity systems, state/local reporting, cross-designation of officers, and money for personnel or detention. Those connections can expand practical reach even when legal authorities remain different.

The **Homeland Security Act was enacted on 25 November 2002**. A contemporary White House account dates the major merger of personnel from **22 organizations to 1 March 2003**. “Twenty-two” describes predecessor organizations contributing personnel/functions, not 22 wholly new agencies. INS functions were divided, with ICE combining immigration and customs enforcement; the organizational event followed enactment. [D01, D20; H27 in the history catalog]

| Component or institution | Role relevant to this research |
|---|---|
| **ICE** | Immigration enforcement, detention/removal and customs-related investigations; civilian law enforcement in DHS. |
| **CBP** | Border, port-of-entry and customs functions. Its participation in interior operations must be identified separately from ICE. |
| **USCIS** | Immigration benefits and naturalization administration; distinct from ICE/CBP enforcement. |
| **EOIR** | Immigration adjudication in **DOJ**, under Attorney General authority. Organizational separation from DHS is not Article III judicial independence. [D03] |
| **FPS** | Federal-property protection; cross-designation can bring other DHS officers into this mission. [D11] |
| **TSA / Secret Service** | Transportation security; protective work and financial investigations, respectively. [D16, D18] |
| **FEMA / CISA** | Disaster assistance and coordination; cybersecurity, infrastructure security and emergency communications. [D15, D17] |
| **Coast Guard** | An **armed service at all times** under 14 U.S.C. §101, including within DHS. This is a crucial exception to any blanket description of DHS as civilian. [D04] |

The immigration division and component names are grounded in the creation statute, contemporary ICE testimony and DHS's indexed component listing [D02, D20, H27]. Some current agency pages were inaccessible. Role descriptions therefore do not certify current staffing, priorities, performance or complete jurisdiction.

The data connection is especially important. Secure Communities could turn an ordinary booking encounter into an immigration check without the local arresting officer becoming an immigration officer. This mechanism differs from 287(g) delegation. GAO's 2012 review found that some people removed under the program lacked a criminal conviction known to ICE; incomplete arrest-charge data constrained further conclusions. Its October 2008–March 2012 cohort is not a current proportion for all ICE activity. A database match, an arrest, a conviction and a lawful removal are separate propositions. [D08]

Reversal was possible, but its scope matters. The 2014 memorandum responded to criticism, litigation and local resistance by changing the named program and enforcement framework. It preserved the fingerprint connection. The 2017 restoration illustrates how retained infrastructure can support a later change of policy. Neither document is offered as an uninterrupted statement of current law. The practical question is therefore both which priorities govern today and which durable capabilities remain available for tomorrow. [D09–D10]

Fusion centers offer a different connection: intelligence coordination rather than a single national police command. The bipartisan Senate investigation found substantial weaknesses in the federal counterterrorism reporting it reviewed, including civil-liberties concerns. It also described privacy review stopping problematic reports, alongside concerns about retention. Its findings cannot be expanded into “every center is useless” or a judicial finding that every disputed record was illegal. State/local ownership, differing missions and the examined reporting period matter. [D06–D07]

Portland supplies a concrete test of shared personnel. Under federal-property authority, DHS brought CBP, ICE and Secret Service officers to assist FPS. OIG found deficient planning, training and equipment coordination. It also found the reviewed uniforms complied with component marking policies, including Police and component identification. Thus this report does **not** support saying all officers were unmarked. The review neither resolves every force/arrest allegation nor guarantees that identification was clear to every person in every encounter. [D11]

The 2025 law adds another mechanism: **$45 billion appropriated for ICE detention capacity**, available through **30 September 2029**, alongside CBP resources. Appropriation is not money already spent or usable beds already delivered. Midway Blitz shows the department publicly describing a joint ICE/CBP operation; inaccessible agency pages limit verification of details here. CPOST's March 2026 original research supplies a critical interpretation, but public perceptions and an author's causal account do not by themselves prove an official hidden motive. No arrest totals or inferred intent are adopted. [D12–D14, D19]

The original Act included privacy and civil-rights offices. Their statutory existence is a starting point, not proof of usable remedies in 2026. For further review, pair every claimed power with an identified constraint: governing authority, written policy, training, data provenance, access to counsel, inspection, judicial remedy and evidence of compliance. Track separately whether a safeguard exists on paper, has staff and access, and has actually interrupted harm. [D20]

This history supports concern about concentrated capacity while preserving distinctions between military status, policing, intelligence, benefits and disaster response. It does not establish a single inevitable plan. It provides a bounded basis for Digest to ask which connections increased coercive reach, which protections weakened or worked, and what evidence is still missing.

# ICE removals, overseas custody and political speech

**RC-006 research for Jared and Digest Aiden.** Evidence cutoff: 10 October 2026 UTC; selected source rechecks on 11 October. This chapter distinguishes documented actions, investigative findings, court decisions and unresolved claims. Source details are in [ice_rights_sources.json](ice_rights_sources.json).

The concern has an evidenced core: removal can transfer a person into another state's coercive system while making location, counsel, medical care and effective review harder to obtain. Political speech has also been the subject of a substantial adverse judicial finding against federal officials. These are consequential institutional facts even without proof that every enforcement program serves the same purpose.

## CECOT: detention abroad, not simply a change of address

Human Rights Watch and Cristosal's November 2025 investigation covers **252 Venezuelans transferred to El Salvador in March–April 2025**. They interviewed 40 former detainees and 150 other knowledgeable people and reviewed records and injury material. The investigators found systematic torture, arbitrary detention and enforced disappearance. These are attributed investigative findings, not a final criminal judgment against the officials involved. The governments alleged gang connections; the investigators found many had no criminal convictions. Neither allegation nor absence of a conviction resolves every individual's case. [R01]

**That cohort was sent to Venezuela on 18 July 2025.** It should not be described as still inside CECOT. Their transfer history remains evidence about the system's capacity and accountability; later departure does not undo earlier harm. Other CECOT populations require separate evidence. [R01]

Kilmar Abrego Garcia's case is distinct. In its **10 April 2025** order, the Supreme Court recorded the government's acknowledgment that his removal to El Salvador violated an existing withholding order. It required facilitation of his release and proper handling of his case, while seeking clarification of the district court's broader direction. This establishes a specific wrongful removal and judicial intervention, not a ruling about every overseas transfer. He returned to the United States in June 2025; the later criminal and immigration proceedings must not be collapsed into the original error. [R11, R12]

For tracking purposes, follow the whole custody chain: which authority ordered removal; what the person was told; the receiving institution; who could locate or contact them; available review; and who could actually secure release. A completed flight is not evidence of safe resettlement or a completed remedy.

## Third-country removals and the latest litigation

HRW's **8 October 2026** report analyzes ICE records showing **536 third-country removals to African countries between 20 January 2025 and 5 August 2026**. Its qualitative investigation interviewed 24 people across six destination countries. It reports detention, ill-treatment, obstacles involving language and support, and onward returns exposing people to persecution or torture. Its ICE and immigration-court datasets could not be linked person by person; the interviews are not a representative prevalence survey. Risk varies by person, destination and receiving arrangement. [R02]

There is an important timing correction. That report was finalized **28 September**. On **29 September 2026**, the Supreme Court stayed the February district-court judgment in **DHS v. D.V.D.** and granted review, with argument directed for December. Questions include jurisdiction, classwide relief and the legality of the government's guidance under immigration law, due process and torture-protection law. A stay changes what relief currently operates; it is **not a final merits ruling that all such removals are lawful**. [R02, R03]

The government's September application argues that third-country removals are necessary when home countries will not accept people and disputes the required process and available judicial jurisdiction. It relies in part on diplomatic assurances. Those are the government's litigation positions, not findings that each assurance protects each person. [R04]

The research test is whether protection is usable before departure: notice in an understood language, time and confidential access to counsel, a meaningful way to raise destination-specific danger, independent assessment, and a mechanism that can halt the transfer. After departure, the test becomes actual monitoring, access and reversibility. Administrative permission and diplomatic agreement alone cannot measure those outcomes.

## Political speech: keep the people and proceedings separate

The Palestinian student or graduate Jared recalls may be **Mahmoud Khalil**, although the description alone is not conclusive. Khalil was a lawful permanent resident when detained in March 2025 after a foreign-policy determination. A later alleged misrepresentation charge is separate and contested. In **January 2026**, the Third Circuit vacated district-court orders on statutory jurisdiction/channeling grounds. That did not decide that retaliation for protected speech is constitutional. [R05]

The Supreme Court's latest entry in the examined extension docket is **17 September 2026**, extending Khalil's petition deadline to **19 October**. An extension is not a grant of review, and the docket does not establish his present custody or removal status. This packet does not claim he was sent to CECOT or a third country. [R06]

**Rümeysa Öztürk is a different person: a Turkish scholar.** Her lawyers announced on **17 April 2026** that, after completing her doctorate, she returned to Türkiye under a settlement ending federal litigation, with a joint request to terminate the immigration appeal. They report government acknowledgment that her student record was reinstated and her status had remained lawful. This is counsel's account; the full settlement and final immigration disposition were not independently audited. It is not evidence of a forced third-country transfer. [R07]

The broader **AAUP v. Rubio** case supplies more than allegations of a political motive. Following a trial, the district court's **30 September 2025** opinion found concerted use of immigration powers to chill protected pro-Palestinian speech. Its **22 January 2026** judgment declared the identified policy unconstitutional and contrary to the Administrative Procedure Act and vacated it. [R08, R09]

Appeal status must accompany that finding. The inspected **7 April 2026** First Circuit order grants an unopposed partial stay specifically of the judgment's **Part IV sanction**. It does not itself vacate the whole judgment or decide the appeal's merits. The case index records later appellate briefing; this packet is not a complete October appellate-docket audit. [R10]

## How this bears on the military concern

The possible mechanism is **classification → coercive custody → transfer → reduced ability to contest or reverse harm**. Recruitment, tactical equipment, detention infrastructure and legal staffing can supply different parts of that mechanism. Their combination deserves examination even when each component has a predecessor. The [capacity](ICE_CAPACITY_AND_OVERSIGHT.md), [personnel](ICE_PERSONNEL_AND_EQUIPMENT.md) and [DHS](DHS_CREATION_AND_USE.md) chapters identify concrete institutional connections.

This evidence does not establish ORA control of ICE or religious selection of its officers. That missing link limits one causal claim; it does not erase independently documented abuse or political targeting. Conversely, an agency's broad lawful mission does not answer whether a particular operation was lawful or whether a remedy worked.

“Legal death sentence” conveys the fear of exposure to fatal harm, but it is not the legal disposition proved by these records. A more testable description is **state-directed transfer into foreseeable danger with impaired access to protection**. Evaluate foreseeability, actual treatment, decision-makers' knowledge and available prevention in each case. The severity of documented abuse does not depend on settling whether CECOT should receive the contested historical label “concentration camp.”

The strongest buffers are usable ones: timely counsel and interpretation, preserved location records, independent medical access, enforceable pauses, safe reception, court compliance and actual return or release. A court victory after an irreversible transfer may arrive too late; a returned person may still need care, documents and protection. Independent organizations can expose failures and support remedies, but their capacity and access should not be assumed unlimited.

**Next bounded collection:** inspect one complete transfer chain and one completed remedy, including contrary evidence and the government's account. Do not infer a population-wide outcome from a handful of cases, and do not wait for population-wide proof before recognizing a substantiated individual violation. No monitor, contact campaign or automated legal-status tracking is activated by this packet.

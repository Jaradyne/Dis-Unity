# Beyond ICE: the institutions to keep in view

**Requested supplement, 11 October 2026 UTC; evidence through the RC-006 cutoff of 10 October.** This map selects U.S. institutions relevant to the military, immigration and domestic-coercion question. It is not a complete census of armed agencies or an assertion that they all act together.

The most consequential omissions from an ICE-only picture would be **CBP, DHS leadership and intelligence coordination, the agencies supplying custody and transport, participating local police, and the separate military command chain**. The map also includes a concrete change outside DHS: direct Interior Secretary supervision of the U.S. Park Police.

## Responsibility map

| Institution | Relevant role | What the research should follow |
|---|---|---|
| **DHS leadership** | Departmental policy, coordination and allocation across components | Directives, delegations, shared resources and who can override a component. See [DHS history](DHS_CREATION_AND_USE.md). |
| **ICE ERO and HSI** | ERO handles immigration custody/removal; HSI investigates a wider set of crimes. Each has tactical capabilities. [I09] | Distinguish the operating component, authority, force records and custody chain. |
| **CBP: Border Patrol and Office of Field Operations** | Separate DHS border-enforcement components, with distinct tactical teams. [I09] | Identify which component acts, including deployments away from routine border work. |
| **Federal Protective Service (FPS)** | Federal-property protection; the Portland history shows the importance of cross-component support. | Identify the property authority, designation, tasking and operational limits. See [DHS history](DHS_CREATION_AND_USE.md). |
| **Coast Guard** | A legally defined armed service, ordinarily operating within DHS; statute provides for operation in the Navy. [A02, A03] | Maritime interception and transfer records; applicable command and legal authority in each operation. |
| **FBI — DOJ** | Criminal investigation and national-security intelligence. [A01] | Evidence collection, classification, information sharing and the legal basis of investigations. |
| **U.S. Marshals Service — DOJ** | Court security, federal orders, fugitive work, prisoner custody and transport. [A01] | Who ordered custody, transport destinations and whether court-directed relief is carried out. |
| **Bureau of Prisons — DOJ** | Federal imprisonment. Its separate facilities have also formed part of ICE's detention-expansion arrangements. [A01, C02] | Distinguish criminal incarceration from immigration custody and identify the governing agreement and standards. |
| **DEA and ATF — DOJ** | Drug-law and firearms/explosives/arson enforcement, respectively. [A01] | Any actual cross-agency task force, deputation or reassignment; mission overlap alone establishes no immigration role. |
| **U.S. Park Police — Interior** | Policing in designated park and other jurisdictions; operational supervision was realigned in 2025. [A04, A05] | Domestic deployments, direct political supervision, orders and review outside the DHS structure. |
| **DoD, active forces and National Guard** | Military support is distinct from civilian police authority; the Guard's duty and command status matter. [A06, A07] | Orders, funding, command, actual tasks, equipment/facility support and the law invoked. |
| **State/local police and sheriffs participating in 287(g)** | Delegated immigration functions under agreements authorized in 1996. [H23] | The actual agreement, deputized staff, training, supervision and complaints; do not assume all local police participate. |
| **USCIS and EOIR** | USCIS immigration-benefit functions are separate from enforcement; DOJ's EOIR conducts administrative immigration adjudication. [H26, A08] | Access, adjudication standards and staffing; an immigration court is not an ICE field unit or an Article III court. |

Agency statements establish stated responsibilities, not successful performance. Historical tactical-team profiles are a baseline, not a 2026 roster. Current deployments need dated orders and operating records.

## One additional authority change worth retaining

**Interior Order 3440, signed 25 August 2025**, directs the Park Police Chief to report to the Secretary or a designee and gives that official strategic management and operational control. It suspends other subordinate officials' supervisory authority while retaining administrative support from the existing offices. The signature-page date controls here; the file name contains September 2, which is not treated as the signing date. [A04]

This is an actual change in reporting authority outside ICE and DHS. Its stated reason is the force's wider jurisdiction and interagency responsibilities. Whether it altered particular deployments, discipline or safeguards requires implementation records. The current general NPS description of Park Police affiliation is not enough to reconstruct that command change. [A04, A05]

## Capabilities that are easy to miss

**Training, information and logistics can matter without making an arrest.** The DHS chapter covers biometric information and fusion centers. Federal training organizations, contractors and shared technology should be linked only where a contract, agreement or operating record identifies their contribution. The Camp East Montana audit is one such connection. [C01]

**Protection and response missions are also part of DHS.** TSA, Secret Service, FEMA and CISA do not become interchangeable immigration forces by sharing a department. Their purposes and the potential movement of staff, data or funds are treated in the DHS chapter. The appropriate question is what authority or resource actually crossed a boundary.

**Military constraints are neither a universal ban nor automatic permission.** The Posse Comitatus statute has express exceptions. The June 2023 CRS account distinguishes federal military service from Guard service under state control and describes CBP support under several administrations. That distinction changes the legal analysis; it does not demonstrate that any particular domestic operation was lawful. Current deployment litigation is outside this bounded inventory. [A06, A07]

**Oversight belongs on the map.** Inspectors general, GAO, courts, congressional committees, civil-rights functions and detention ombudsman arrangements are different checks. DOJ's Civil Rights Division and OIG have their own responsibilities. [A01] The detention oversight chapter shows why a statutory office's existence and a usable remedy must be tracked separately. [C05]

## How to examine the network without inventing a single hidden command

For each consequential operation, collect one row covering **decision-maker, participating agencies, legal authority, money, data, force, custody, transport, oversight and outcome**. Mark unknown fields. An actual interagency agreement or shared command order establishes a connection; similar clothing, rhetoric or employment backgrounds do not.

Then ask whether the arrangement makes coercive power easier to use and correction harder to obtain. Splitting responsibility can make accountability difficult even without a secret agreement. Coordination can also support rescue, protect people or enforce a remedy. The observed outcome and the effective ability to challenge it should decide the assessment.

**Remaining scope gaps:** a full intelligence-community history, every federal tactical unit, private-contractor ownership networks, overseas military bases, every state task force and all current Guard-deployment lawsuits. These are named gaps, not presumed absent actors. The next research should follow a material connection found here rather than expanding indefinitely by agency name.

# ICE capacity and effective oversight

**Evidence cutoff: 10 October 2026 UTC; selected sources rechecked 11 October.** RC-006 follow-up by `ice_operations_recovery`. Sources: [C01–C05](ice_capacity_sources.json); [dated entries](ice_capacity_events.json). These are selected public audits and appropriations, not a complete operational census.

**The military connection includes actual contracting and detention infrastructure.** The concern is therefore more concrete than similar uniforms or personnel backgrounds. But appropriated money, constructed space and usable detention capacity are different quantities. Scrutiny should follow both expanding coercive capacity and the ability to detect, stop and remedy harm.

## Money and usable capacity

The July 2025 law added **$45 billion for detention** and **$29.85 billion for broader ICE enforcement purposes**, available through September 2029. These are additional multiyear appropriations, not annual spending or proof of expenditure. [C03]

The June 10, 2026 law separately added **$31.075 billion for immigration enforcement** and **$7.45 billion for HSI activities excluding immigration and customs enforcement**, also available through September 2029. Treating the latter entirely as a removal budget would erase a statutory distinction. [C04]

Keep the resource ledger separated:

| Resource | Evidence needed to establish usable capacity |
|---|---|
| Capital | Completed facilities, utilities, secure access and accepted construction. |
| Cash | Purpose-restricted appropriations, obligations, actual payments and remaining balances. |
| Staff | Qualified personnel actually on shift, including clinical, interpretation and legal-access support. |
| Equipment | Delivered, working and maintainable inventory with trained operators. |
| Beds | Open, staffed, inspected places with necessary services; authorized or advertised beds alone are insufficient. |

These categories can constrain each other. Empty beds do not prove harmlessness; inadequate staffing or care can make detention more dangerous even at lower occupancy. Conversely, large appropriations cannot be read as instantaneous, fully functioning capacity. Spending pressure may produce both expansion and waste at the same time.

## What the audits show

**Camp East Montana:** GAO found that the Army awarded and administered a **$1.3 billion** Fort Bliss detention contract; the facility opened in August 2025 and administration passed to ICE that October. ICE missed its required pre-occupancy inspection. Medical and sanitation deficiencies followed. Contracted meals/services covered 5,000 people despite approximately 1,600 detainees at February 2026's end. All four recommendations remained open in the reviewed status. [C01]

**Expansion:** GAO's September 24 report cites ICE's average-daily-population measure increasing from **39,314 on January 20, 2025 to 67,180 on July 30, 2026**. Authorized facilities rose **134→272** by July 23; authorization does not mean every site was operating. Guantanamo's initial 5,000 tent beds were dismantled unused; its FY2026 running average was 16 detainees through June 18. ICE projected a strategic plan by **August 31, 2027**—a future commitment, not completed planning. [C02]

These examples demonstrate why the relevant military link is a traceable institutional relationship: who contracted, paid, operated and inspected. A nominal separation of agencies cannot answer how their combined resources functioned. Equally, one facility's failures cannot establish the condition of every facility.

## An office in law versus an effective remedy

GAO's May 2025 oversight review identified inadequate performance goals and measures. Its May 2026 status records DHS saying an April 30 appropriation eliminated OIDO; GAO responds that the Immigration Detention Ombudsman remains established under **6 U.S.C. §205**. All three recommendations remained open. This is an attributed institutional disagreement, not a judicial resolution or evidence of a functioning complaint office. [C05]

For Digest, test the complete remedy: can a detained person obtain confidential contact, interpretation and counsel; is the complaint recorded; can an independent reviewer inspect relevant evidence; and does a substantiated finding produce timely correction without retaliation? Counting offices, inspections or passing ratings cannot answer those questions by itself.

The next bounded comparison should pair facility occupancy with staffing and care deficiencies, then follow one complaint from receipt through corrective action. Public aggregate staffing, inspection follow-up and contract-performance records can reveal deterioration before catastrophic outcomes, while retaining unknowns about conditions the records do not measure.

# Postwar foundations, 1945–2003

**Prepared for Jared and Digest; evidence cutoff 10 October 2026 UTC.** This is a selective institutional timeline, not a complete history of the U.S. military or immigration. Every entry is a **late discovery for this packet**, not a newly occurring event. The original interrupted specialist checkpoint remains intact; this report is the separately attributed `postwar_recovery` continuation.

The strongest historical connection is practical: military resources, civilian enforcement, loyalty screening and immigration selection have repeatedly been joined through specific authorities. The record also contains deliberate separation, expanded rights, investigation and repeal. Those countercurrents show where restraint can operate; they do not establish that restraint works automatically.

| Date | Milestone and relevance | Constraint or unresolved question |
|---|---|---|
| **1945** | The UN Charter articulates rights without religious distinction. The Nuremberg charter rejects immunity based on official position or superior orders; the major trial begins **20 November**. [H01–H03] | These create an accountability baseline, not proof that postwar states consistently followed it. |
| **21 March 1947** | **EO 9835** makes loyalty review part of federal civilian employment, drawing on FBI, military and other files. [H04] | It provides notice, counsel, hearings and administrative appeals, while permitting secrecy-qualified disclosure and suspension. Neither “no appeal” nor “fully fair process” follows. |
| **26 July 1947** | The **National Security Act** creates the NSC and CIA and coordinates military departments under civilian control. [H05] | The original CIA charter expressly excludes police, law-enforcement and internal-security functions. The enactment date is **26 July**, not 27 July. |
| **26 July 1948** | **EO 9981** orders military equality without regard to race, color, religion or national origin, with an implementation committee. [H06] | The archival account records resistance; an order and its implementation are different evidence. |
| **5 May 1950 → 31 May 1951** | The **UCMJ** is enacted, then takes effect, consolidating military justice and establishing civilian appellate judges. [H07] | Creates a review institution; does not prove every commander or proceeding respected it. |
| **23 September 1950** | The **Internal Security Act** includes emergency-detention provisions. [H08–H09] | Its later repeal is verified. Original Title II was inaccessible here, so detailed powers and actual use remain outside this review. |
| **27 June → 24 December 1952** | The **INA** is enacted and becomes effective. It preserves national-origins quotas while ending racial barriers to naturalization. [H10–H11] | Inclusion and exclusion change together. Official history documents the Cold War security debate; it does not establish a single motive for every provision. |
| **1954** | INS conducts the deportation initiative officially called **Operation Wetback**. The historical name contains a slur. [H12] | Verified here only through an official indexed historical excerpt. No contested mass-removal total is adopted. Deportation capacity clearly predates ICE. |
| **17 August 1955** | **EO 10631**, the Code of Conduct, includes God language and addresses behavior in combat and captivity. [H13] | The displayed codification includes later amendments. Its stated scope differs from a general religious test for promotion. |
| **17 January 1961** | Eisenhower warns that the permanent military establishment and arms industry can acquire unwarranted political influence. [H14] | A contemporaneous warning about institutional incentives, not proof of a later unified conspiracy. |
| **3 October 1965** | **Hart–Celler** revises immigration selection, replacing national-origins allocation with a new preference structure. [H15] | Numerical ceilings and other exclusion rules remain. Reform is substantial without being complete. |
| **15 June 1970** | **Welsh** recognizes conscientious objection grounded in deeply held moral or ethical convictions beyond conventional religious labeling. [H16] | Concerns opposition to war in any form; not a right to refuse every disputed policy. The opinions reach the result through different reasoning. |
| **25 September 1971** | The **Non-Detention Act** repeals the 1950 emergency-detention title and requires congressional authority for federal detention of citizens. [H09] | A real reversal, but neither an absolute detention ban nor a general immigration safeguard for noncitizens. |
| **26 June 1972** | **Laird v. Tatum** rejects the Army-surveillance challenge on standing: subjective chill alone is insufficient. [H17] | Access to judicial review can fail before the underlying practice receives a merits ruling. The decision does not approve all military surveillance. |
| **1975–1978** | The **Church investigation**, its 1976 report and permanent Senate oversight, followed by **FISA in 1978**, expose abuses and build review institutions. [H18–H19] | FISA covers defined surveillance and contains exceptions. These reforms demonstrate repair, not permanent immunity from abuse. |
| **17 March 1980** | The **Refugee Act** regularizes admissions/resettlement around persecution-based protection and consultation. [H20] | Protection exists within the same state that enforces exclusion; usable access depends on policy, resources and implementation. |
| **1 December 1981** | Military cooperation legislation facilitates civilian enforcement support. **GAO's 1983 review** observes increased military participation in drug interdiction and coordination problems. [H21] | GAO also describes readiness and direct-involvement limits. This is a documented resource bridge, not merger of military and police authority. |
| **23 September 1996** | **NDAA §1033** authorizes transfer of suitable excess defense property, including arms, to federal/state law-enforcement agencies. [H22] | Authorization is distinct from an actual transfer, its recipient, training or use. |
| **30 September 1996** | **IIRIRA** adds **287(g)** delegation, expedited removal and a closed-military-base detention pilot. [H23] | Training/supervision and fear/asylum referral are specified. Different clauses have different effective dates; Title III-A generally starts **1 April 1997**. Pilot implementation remains unverified. |
| **18 September 2001** | The **AUMF** authorizes military force against specified actors connected to the September 11 attacks and those harboring them. [H24] | It is not itself ICE's creation or a general immigration-policing authorization. |
| **26 October 2001** | **PATRIOT §412** creates security-certified noncitizen detention, with a seven-day proceedings/charge requirement, periodic review and specified habeas review. [H25] | A legal mechanism's existence does not establish its prevalence or cover every immigration detention. |
| **25 November 2002** | The **Homeland Security Act** creates DHS and divides legacy immigration functions, abolishing INS after transfers. [H26] | Enactment, organizational effectiveness and actual transfers occur on different dates. |
| **1 March 2003** | **ICE forms** by combining legacy immigration and customs enforcement capabilities. Contemporary leadership testimony explicitly describes their combined power. [H27] | ICE is civilian law enforcement in DHS; its military relationships must be traced through personnel, resources, authorities and operations. |

The distinction between an institutional link and an unsupported historical analogy matters. The 1981 support changes, 1996 equipment authority, delegated immigration work and closed-base detention provision are identifiable links. They establish channels through which capabilities can combine. They do not establish that every recipient used the same equipment, that veterans retain military command relationships in civilian employment, or that religious programs caused immigration decisions.

Three questions carry forward into the ICE research. **Who can act?** Track changes in appointing authority, delegated powers and command responsibility. **What can they use?** Separate equipment, staff, transport, detention facilities, money and shared data. **Who can interrupt or reverse harm?** Track access to counsel, complaint handling, independent inspection, judicial review and actual compliance with remedies. A safeguard is useful evidence only at the level demonstrated: words, an operating process, or a successful intervention.

This history supports taking cumulative risk seriously without assuming an inevitable trajectory from 1945 to the present. Capacity can accumulate across otherwise separate decisions. Equally, abolition, rights expansion, disclosure and independent review can redirect it. The useful test is whether contemporary changes strengthen coercive capacity while weakening a person's ability to refuse, contest classification, secure release or obtain a remedy.

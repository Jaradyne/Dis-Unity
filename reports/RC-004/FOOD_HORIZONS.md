# Food across response horizons — RC-004

Evidence cutoff: 21 September 2026. Bounded public-source research by food_horizons. No inventory audit, land access, water supply or food commitment is established.

The useful question is whether food already available can last until the next dependable delivery or harvest. A food bank's normal stock turnover, the shelf life of its products, and the number of people its accessible stock could serve are different measurements. A month of turnover does not mean everything spoils after a month, nor that the institution can feed everyone for a month after replenishment stops.

## Four overlapping layers

| Planning layer | What can carry the load | What must be established |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate through several days | Food and water already accessible; existing kitchens and distribution | Usable stock, preparation, opening hours and actual reach |
| Days through weeks | Rotating shelf-stable food, protected cold storage and alternate deliveries | Cover at stressed demand; cooking water/fuel; next arrival; staff and transport |
| Weeks through months | Successive seasonal crops, established seedlings and regional harvests | Current crop stage, edible yield, water, soil fertility, harvest labor and losses |
| Seasons onward | Crop calendars, preservation/storage, seed and nursery capacity, nutrient return | Replenishment under shared disruptions and the donor's protected local supply |

These are planning bands, not measured reserve durations. Stored food and growing crops should overlap: a garden planted on the first day of a supply interruption cannot be counted as immediate food. Preserve variety, protein and calories rather than measuring success solely in pounds of produce.

## Growing food changes the dependence; it does not eliminate it

Tomatoes can be productive, but are not a quick all-season emergency staple. UC IPM identifies them as warm-weather plants; it lists approximate planting in May for Monterey County north, versus December–March in the desert valleys. Seed starts may precede planting by four to six weeks. Those are planting windows, not harvest dates, and do not establish an outdoor December tomato reserve in Oakland. Cool-season radishes and successive plantings offer a different seasonal role. [RC4A03](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/cultural-tips-for-growing-tomato/) [RC4A04](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/home-and-landscape/cultural-tips-for-growing-radish/)

Small plots can supply freshness, dietary variety, skills, seedlings and social cooperation. They should not be credited with replacing regional staple supply without a yield-and-diet calculation. Growing calorie and protein crops, preserving seasonal abundance and exchanging with larger nearby producers belong alongside fast greens, rather than being silently represented by a tomato garden.

A December route from a warmer region to a frozen one is a useful conditional network: verify actual crop availability, surplus after local needs, packing, suitable temperature control, passable roads, drivers, energy, unloading and recipient access. A refrigerated vehicle may need to protect produce from freezing in winter. The right cargo may be seasonal vegetables, stored roots or preserved foods rather than fresh tomatoes. No operating corridor or spare delivery capacity was verified here. This approach does not presume mass migration.

## Oakland's compost loop has a real access point

The City lists free compost year-round at **Acta Non Verba's West Oakland Farm Park, 2847 Peralta Street**. At retrieval, hours were Tuesday–Friday 1–6 pm and Saturday 10 am–2 pm. Bring containers and load it yourself. The City says the compost originates in food scraps and yard waste collected from Oakland homes and businesses. It also says supply can run out between deliveries, particularly during high demand or wet weather. [RC4A01](https://www.oaklandca.gov/My-Household/Waste-and-Recycling/Free-Compost)

This makes a practical near-term project possible: a shared pickup and delivery for growers lacking a vehicle. It requires an available compost pile and willing participants, not a new institution. Modifiable space with suitable growing containers, clean soil, water and seasonal plants can be enough to begin; agreements and infrastructure can develop with use.

WM describes residential green/food-waste processing at Altamont, with an advertised design capacity up to 500 tons/day. Its undated page retains historical future-tense language, so this is evidence of facility design, not present output or spare capacity. It does not by itself prove the present destination of every Oakland green-bin stream. Residential, multifamily and commercial routing should be checked separately. [RC4A02](https://www.wm.com/us/en/facilities/altamont-landfill/composting)

Compost is a useful buffer, but volume is not nutrient sufficiency. UC's nutrient guidance explains that organic nitrogen becomes available at rates controlled by decomposition, carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, soil moisture and temperature. Compost applied solely to satisfy nitrogen needs can overapply phosphorus and potassium. Soil tests and compost analysis help match supply to the crop. Legume cover crops can add nitrogen with suitable biology and time; recycling cannot create new phosphorus or potassium to replace continuing exports. [RC4A05](https://ucanr.edu/site/solution-center-nutrient-management/nitrogen-organic-sources)

For a new urban growing site, checking soil history and obtaining appropriate soil testing is a prudent proposed first step; this cycle did not establish the condition of any site. Clean growing media in containers can separate an initial project from uncertain ground conditions. Neither method guarantees water or nutrients throughout a season.

## Why measure local service capacity?

The purpose is to learn how much waiting a network can absorb and who can actually reach it. A busy regional warehouse is not the same as frequent distribution within walking distance. The Bay Area demonstration does not establish that it is the region with greatest need. Rural Tennessee may require a different design—longer delivery routes, less frequent distribution, transport pools and larger household carryover—but county-specific evidence is needed before comparing adequacy.

Record usable service cover, replenishment time, distribution frequency, travel requirements and restrictions separately. If food exists but people cannot reach it, the intervention may be transport or access. If incoming supply is absent, a larger queue or more meal preparation cannot solve the missing food. If the gap lasts long enough, production and preservation become increasingly important.

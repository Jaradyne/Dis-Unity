# Backhaul Field Question — not a new invention

## Established baseline

Backhaul optimization is already standard freight practice.

FHWA defines **deadhead** as an empty backhaul and describes empty miles as extremely important to carrier efficiency. FHWA also documents 3PL/shipping practices that deliberately fill otherwise empty return truck movements with inbound commodities.

Commercial load boards such as DAT explicitly sell backhaul/trihaul/reload tools to reduce deadhead.

Therefore Dis-Unity should **not** ask truckers:
> "Has anyone ever considered carrying something back?"

That would sound naive.

## Better Question

Ask:

> **Where do useful return loads still fail to match because the relevant community/municipal/agricultural needs are not visible inside ordinary freight markets?**

Examples:
- small rural communities below normal load-board scale;
- public/commons material flows;
- repairables/containers;
- farm outputs timed around inbound essentials;
- disaster/emergency return routes;
- community-scale aggregation too small for a normal broker;
- incompatible timing/equipment that might become viable if demand is bundled.

## Field-community use

Public trucker forums/Reddit can be useful for:
- driver/operator experience;
- why a theoretically good backhaul is impractical;
- detention/timing/equipment realities;
- broker payment risk;
- whether proposed triangular routes are annoying or genuinely useful.

Treat these as ground-report leads, not prevalence evidence.

A good public prompt would be:

> "Backhauls are obviously standard practice. I'm interested in edge cases where a town or rural corridor has real outbound needs/material but they're too fragmented or invisible to normal load boards. What usually prevents those from becoming viable return freight — timing, aggregation, equipment, payment, broker risk, something else?"

Do not solicit private customer/route information people should not disclose.

## Public-sector bridge

Some jurisdictions have emergency/procurement mechanisms that can contract for urgent goods/services, but authority varies by law, emergency declaration and local rules.

For California examples:
- Cal OES describes local emergency proclamations as activating extraordinary emergency provisions and potentially state/federal assistance;
- Caltrans uses emergency contracts for immediate threats to life, health, property or essential public services;
- California DGS provides statewide/leveraged procurement agreements local governments may sometimes use.

A City Council vote is therefore **not a universal magic switch**. The Carrier Lifeline Bee should identify the actual local authority/procurement mechanism before proposing it.
